Keynes’s question

The problem

  • The Great Depression brought persistent unemployment and sharply lower output.
  • Many people were willing to work, but firms were not producing or hiring enough.
  • Lower wages alone did not guarantee more jobs: firms also needed customers for the goods additional workers would make.

Keynes’s intuition

  • Firms respond to weak sales by cutting production.
  • Workers then receive less income and households spend less, weakening sales further.
  • An economy can therefore remain below its productive capacity in the short run.

The question for this lecture: how do consumption and investment spending determine output, and what happens when planned spending changes?

Our starting model

We begin with households and firms in a closed economy without government. Households buy consumption goods. Firms buy new capital goods and add to inventories.

\[ \boxed{Y=C+I.} \]

Here \(Y\) is the value of output and the income it generates, \(C\) is consumption, and \(I\) is actual investment, including changes in inventories. This is an accounting identity for this simplified economy.

Firms also make plans. Their planned investment \(I^P\) may differ from actual investment when sales differ from expectations. We therefore study planned spending \(AE=C+I^P\) and ask when it equals output.

What determines consumption?

Consumption depends on more than current income. A broader relationship is

\[ C=C(Y_D,r,W,Y_D^e). \]

  • \(C\): household consumption of goods and services.
  • \(Y_D\): disposable income, income available after taxes.
  • \(r\): the real interest rate, relevant to borrowing and saving.
  • \(W\): wealth, the value of assets less liabilities.
  • \(Y_D^e\): expected future disposable income.

Higher current income normally raises consumption. Greater wealth or stronger expected income can also support spending. For now, with no taxes, \(Y_D=Y\); we hold \(r\), \(W\) and \(Y_D^e\) fixed.

The consumption function

Keynes’s intuition was that consumption rises with income, but by less than the full increase in income. We represent that idea with a straight line:

\[ \boxed{C=a+bY=\bar C+bY},\qquad 0<b<1. \]

  • \(C\): real consumption; \(Y\): real current income.
  • \(a=\bar C\): autonomous consumption, the intercept when \(Y=0\). The bar means we hold this part fixed as income changes.
  • \(b\): the marginal propensity to consume (MPC), or slope. It measures how much consumption rises when income increases by one unit.

For example, \(C=50+0.5Y\) has autonomous consumption of 50. A household could finance consumption at zero current income using savings or borrowing. A rise in wealth or expected income changes the intercept, shifting the whole function.

Marginal propensity to consume

\[ MPC=b=\frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y}. \]

If \(C=50+0.5Y\), another £1 of income raises consumption by 50p. The other 50p is saved.

Income \(Y\) Consumption \(C\) Change in consumption
100 100 —
120 110 £10 for £20 more income

Thus \(MPC=10/20=0.5\). The MPC is the slope, not the fraction of total income consumed.

Consumption function

In \(C=50+0.5Y\), the intercept is \(\bar C=50\) and the slope is \(MPC=0.5\). A change in \(Y\) moves us along the line; a change in autonomous consumption shifts it.

Spending versus saving

At each level of income, households either consume or save:

\[ Y=C+S \quad\Rightarrow\quad S=Y-C. \]

Since \(\bar C=a\) is consumption independent of current income, it reduces saving by the same amount. Substituting \(C=\bar C+bY\) gives

\[ S=-\bar C+(1-b)Y. \]

At very low current income, saving can be negative.

This is dissaving: households consume more than their current income.

In our example, \(Y=40\) gives \(C=70\) and \(S=-30\); at \(Y=160\), \(C=130\) and \(S=30\).

Marginal propensity to save

The marginal propensity to save (MPS) is the part of an additional unit of income that households save. Because every extra pound is either consumed or saved,

\[ \Delta Y=\Delta C+\Delta S. \]

Dividing through by \(\Delta Y\) gives

\[ MPS=\frac{\Delta S}{\Delta Y}=1-b=1-MPC. \]

In our example, income rises from 100 to 120. Consumption rises from 100 to 110, so saving rises from 0 to 10. Thus \(MPC=10/20=0.5\) and \(MPS=10/20=0.5\).

The average propensity to consume asks a different question: what share of total income is consumed? At \(Y=120\), \(APC=C/Y=110/120\approx0.917\), while the MPC remains 0.5.

Saving function

For \(C=50+0.5Y\), the saving function is \(S=-50+0.5Y\).

Its intercept is \(-50\) and its slope is \(MPS=0.5\).

Saving becomes zero at \(Y=100\), where consumption exactly equals income.

Below that income, households dissave; above it, saving is positive.

  • A rise in wealth or expected future income can shift consumption upward.
  • At a given current income, saving then falls by the same amount.

Investment in this model

In national accounts, investment is spending on newly produced capital goods, residential construction and additions to inventories.

  • A firm buying a new machine adds to productive capital.
  • Building a new house counts as residential investment.
  • Goods produced but not sold this period add to inventories.

\[ I=I(r, Y, technology) \]

Investment in this model

In national accounts, investment is spending on newly produced capital goods, residential construction and additions to inventories.

  • A firm buying a new machine adds to productive capital.
  • Building a new house counts as residential investment.
  • Goods produced but not sold this period add to inventories.

\[ I=I(r, Y, technology) \]

Buying a share in an existing firm is a financial transaction; it does not itself produce a new machine or house.

Planned investment responds to expected sales, technology and the cost of finance.

We first hold those influences fixed, writing \(I^P=\bar I\), where the bar indicates a fixed level of planned investment.

Autonomous planned investment

The flat line is an assumption: changes in current income do not directly change planned investment here.

Firms can still revise their plans, moving the whole line.

Autonomous investment \(I=\bar{I}\)

Investment and the interest rate

A higher real interest rate makes borrowing dearer and raises the return an investment project must earn. Fewer projects may be worthwhile.

\[ I^P=I_0-\gamma r,\qquad \gamma>0. \]

  • \(I_0\) is planned investment at a zero real interest rate
  • \(-\gamma=\Delta I^P/\Delta r\) is the change in planned investment for a one-unit change in the interest-rate measure.
  • This slope is not an elasticity. We use \(\gamma\) so that \(b\) continues to mean the MPC.

The straight-line equation gives one simple approximation. The next graph allows sensitivity to vary along the schedule. When solving for output later, we hold \(r\) fixed, so \(I^P=\bar I\).

Investment schedule

When the real interest rate rises, fewer projects cover their financing cost. The movement from 3% to 6% in the graph reduces planned investment from 100 to 60. A better profit outlook shifts the schedule to the right: firms plan more investment at the same interest rate.

Planned and actual investment

Firms decide how much to produce before they know exactly how much customers will buy. The resulting inventory change can therefore differ from their plan.

\[ I^A=I^P+\Delta\text{inventories}^{U},\qquad \Delta\text{inventories}^{U}=Y-AE. \]

  • \(I^P\): planned investment, including intended additions to inventories.
  • \(\Delta\text{inventories}^{U}\): the unplanned change in inventories when sales differ from plans.
  • \(I^A\): actual investment, including both planned and unplanned changes.

If \(AE>Y\), firms sell more than they produced and inventories fall unexpectedly.

If \(AE<Y\), inventories rise unexpectedly. These signals help firms decide whether to raise or cut output.

Planned aggregate expenditure

Planned aggregate expenditure (\(AE\)) is the amount households and firms intend to buy at each level of income. Substitute their behavioural equations into \(AE=C+I^P\):

\[ C=\bar C+bY,\qquad I^P=\bar I, \] \[ \boxed{AE(Y)=\underbrace{\bar C+\bar I}_{\text{autonomous spending}}+\underbrace{bY}_{\text{induced consumption}}.} \]

This expression gives planned demand at any level of output. \(\bar C+\bar I\) is spending independent of current income. The term \(bY\) rises with income; its slope is the MPC, \(b\).

At equilibrium, output supplied equals planned demand:

\[ \underbrace{Y}_{\text{output supplied}} =\underbrace{(\bar C+\bar I)+bY}_{\text{planned demand for goods}}. \]

This expenditure schedule is not the aggregate-demand curve relating a price level to output.

From consumption to expenditure

Equilibrium output

Goods-market equilibrium requires firms’ output to match planned purchases:

\[ Y=AE=C+I^P. \]

On a Keynesian cross, the 45-degree line records \(AE=Y\). Equilibrium lies where the planned-expenditure line crosses it.

If \(AE>Y\), inventories fall unexpectedly and firms have an incentive to produce more. If \(AE<Y\), inventories rise unexpectedly and firms tend to cut production.

The Keynesian cross

Away from equilibrium

Suppose output is \(Y=100\). Planned spending is \(70+0.5(100)=120\).

Customers want more than firms produce, so inventories fall unexpectedly by 20 and firms expand.

At \(Y=180\), planned spending is \(160\).

Unsold goods add 20 to inventories; firms tend to reduce production.

These inventory signals move output towards the intersection.

In reality the adjustment takes time; the diagram captures its direction.

Inventory adjustment

Numerical example

Recall the general relationships before substituting numbers:

\[ AE(Y)=\bar C+bY+\bar I,\qquad \Delta\text{inventories}^{U}=Y-AE(Y),\qquad I^A=I^P+\Delta\text{inventories}^{U}. \]

Now let \(\bar C=50\), \(b=0.5\), \(\bar I=20\), and initial output \(Y_0=100\). Planned spending is

\[ AE(Y_0)=\bar C+bY_0+\bar I=50+0.5(100)+20=120. \]

Firms sell 20 more than expected, so unplanned inventories fall by 20:

\[ \Delta\text{inventories}^{U}=Y_0-AE_0=100-120=-20. \]

With \(I^P=20\), actual investment is \(I^A=20-20=0\). Firms have an incentive to raise output. What happens to consumption as income rises?

The first rounds of adjustment

The initial spending gap tells firms how much more to produce. The higher output becomes household income, which changes consumption according to the MPC:

\[ \Delta Y_1=AE(Y_0)-Y_0=20,\qquad \Delta C_1=b\Delta Y_1=0.5(20)=10. \]

Planned spending therefore rises again: \(AE(Y_1)=AE(Y_0)+\Delta C_1=130\).

Round Output \(Y\) Planned expenditure \(AE=70+0.5Y\) Gap \(AE-Y\)
Initial 100 120 20
After first response 120 130 10
After second response 130 135 5
After third response 135 137.5 2.5

The gap shrinks because each additional round of consumption is only half the preceding increase in income.

Equilibrium by substitution

At equilibrium, \(Y=AE(Y)\). Write the general equation first, then substitute the numbers:

\[ Y=\bar C+bY+\bar I \quad\Rightarrow\quad Y=50+0.5Y+20. \]

\[ 0.5Y=70\quad\Rightarrow\quad Y^*=140. \]

Check: \(C(140)=120\), \(I^P=20\), and \(AE=120+20=140\). Planned purchases exactly match output. Unplanned inventory investment is zero.

Saving and investment at equilibrium

Household income is either consumed or saved: \(Y=C+S\).

Goods-market equilibrium requires planned purchases to equal output: \(Y=C+I^P\).

Subtract \(C\) from both equalities:

\[ \boxed{S=I^P \quad\text{at goods-market equilibrium}.} \]

At other output levels, actual investment can still equal saving in the national accounts because actual investment includes unplanned inventory changes.

The condition here concerns planned investment.

Saving equals planned investment

Using the worked example, \(S=-50+0.5Y\) and \(I^P=20\).

Their intersection is at \(Y=140\), the same output as in the Keynesian cross.

Equilibrium formula

The numerical example gave \(Y^*=140\).

To solve any version of this simple model, begin with the equilibrium condition and collect the terms in \(Y\):

\[ Y=\bar C+bY+\bar I \quad\Rightarrow\quad (1-b)Y=\bar C+\bar I. \]

\[ \boxed{Y^*=\frac{1}{1-b}\,(\bar C+\bar I).} \]

The bracket is autonomous planned spending. The factor \(1/(1-b)\) tells us how much equilibrium output corresponds to one unit of that spending.

With \(\bar C=50\), \(\bar I=20\), and \(b=0.5\), it gives \(Y^*=2(70)=140\).

A change in planned investment

Keep \(\bar C=50\) and \(b=0.5\), but let firms raise their fixed planned investment from \(\bar I_0=20\) to \(\bar I_1=40\). Define its change before using it:

\[ \Delta\bar I\equiv\bar I_1-\bar I_0=40-20=20. \]

The bar means this part of investment does not depend on current income in our model. A rise in \(\bar I\) shifts planned expenditure up by 20 at every \(Y\):

\[ AE_0(Y)=70+0.5Y,\qquad AE_1(Y)=90+0.5Y. \]

We will calculate the new equilibrium and then see the shift in the final diagram.

Repeated spending rounds

At the old equilibrium \(Y_0^*=140\), the extra investment first raises planned spending by 20. When firms produce the additional goods, income rises, generating more consumption:

\[ \Delta Y=20+0.5(20)+0.5^2(20)+\cdots =20+10+5+2.5+\cdots. \]

Each round is half the previous one because \(MPC=b=0.5\). This short calculation makes the multiplier mechanism visible without following every inventory adjustment indefinitely.

In general, \(\Delta Y=\Delta\bar I(1+b+b^2+\cdots)\). Since \(0<b<1\), the sequence converges.

Multiplier formula

The geometric sum gives the change in equilibrium output:

\[ \boxed{\Delta Y^*=\frac{1}{1-b}\,\Delta\bar I =\frac{1}{MPS}\,\Delta\bar I.} \]

Here \(b=0.5\), so the multiplier is \(1/(1-0.5)=2\).

Thus \(\Delta Y^*=2(20)=40\), and the new equilibrium is \(Y_1^*=140+40=180\).

Check with the general level formula: \(Y_1^*=[1/(1-0.5)](50+40)=180\). Both methods give the same result.
